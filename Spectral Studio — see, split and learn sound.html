    How to read a spectrogram

    A spectrogram is a picture of sound: time runs left → right, pitch / frequency runs bottom → top, and brightness is loudness. A waveform shows you how the air pressure moves, but a spectrogram shows you which frequencies are present, which is much closer to what your ear separates.

    • Horizontal lines = steady pitched tones. One line alone is a pure sine.
    • Evenly-stacked lines (at f, 2f, 3f…) = one pitched note with harmonics. The bottom line is the note you hear; the pattern of the others is the timbre. Turn on the Harmonic ruler (H) in the Analyzer to check which lines belong together.
    • Vertical lines / stripes = sudden hits (transients): drums, plucks, consonants.
    • Diffuse clouds = noise: hi-hats, cymbals, breath, “s” sounds, reverb tails.
    • Curving lines = pitch moving: slides, vibrato (wiggles), pitch drops (kick drums, lasers).
    • Smearing to the right = reverb or echo.

    The resolution trade-off

    The FFT size decides how the picture is cut up. A large FFT (4096–8192) gives sharp horizontal lines, so you can read bass notes and chords, but drum hits get blurry in time. A small FFT (512) makes drum hits sharp but blurs the pitches together. Try switching it while looking at the demo song.

    Decomposing a song: a workflow

    1. Load a song and analyse a 20–60 second section.
    2. Run Auto-split to get a starting point (tonal vs. hits × low/mid/high).
    3. Solo each layer and listen. Rename the layers by what you actually hear.
    4. Zoom into a bar. Use the Harmonic ruler on a bass note and paint it into a “Bass” layer with the harmonics brush.
    5. Erase what belongs elsewhere. Use Loop view to repeat a bar while you refine it.
    6. Switch between Original ↔ Layers mix (A key). Whatever ends up in “Everything else” is what you haven't explained yet.
    7. To understand a sound you've isolated, rebuild it in the Sound Lab: match the waveform, the harmonics and the envelope.

    Why do some parts look blurry? (it is all sine waves)

    Every sound can be built by adding up sine waves, each with its own frequency, strength and starting phase. A spectrogram tries to show which sines are present at each moment. The catch is that to measure a frequency you have to watch the wave for a while. A 100 Hz sine only repeats every 10 ms, so you need at least a few of those cycles to be sure it is 100 Hz and not 105 Hz.

    • Steady tone → thin horizontal line. The sine stays the same, so a long window measures it precisely.
    • Short event → vertical smear. A 5 ms blip of a 1 kHz sine is mathematically the same as a whole band of sines that cancel each other out everywhere except during those 5 ms. So the blur is real content, not a display error. The shorter the event, the wider the smear (Δt × Δf ≈ constant: the uncertainty principle).
    • Noise → cloud. Hiss is thousands of sines at every frequency with random phases and constantly changing strengths. There are no lines to find because there are no steady sines.
    • Window blur. Even a perfect sine spreads over a few neighbouring FFT bins, because the analysis window cuts it off at the edges. Bigger FFT = narrower spread, but blurrier timing.

    Vertical stripes that follow a pitch curve

    Voices, saws and bowed strings make sound in pulses: the vocal cords snap shut once per cycle. With a small FFT (a short window, shorter than one cycle) the spectrogram can see each pulse separately, so you get a vertical stripe once per period. The stripes are spaced 1/f apart (5 ms at 200 Hz), so when the pitch rises they crowd together and when it falls they spread out. With a large FFT the same sound shows as horizontal harmonic lines instead. Both pictures are correct; they just ask different questions. Select a bit of a vocal with ⇔ Time sel.: the analysis reports “repeats every … ms” and draws a white tick at each period.

    A thin sliver that holds most of the sound

    Colours use decibels, a log scale: every 10 dB step is 10× the energy, so a thin bright line can easily hold more energy than the whole faint haze around it. Switch Spectrum → Brightness to Energy (linear) to see where the energy really is, or draw a ⬚ Box sel. around the sliver: the analysis tells you what % of the energy it holds. Reassigned mode squeezes the blur back towards the true sines, so lines and hits get thin.
